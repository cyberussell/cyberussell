# Purpose

Think with AI is the most important Pillar in the Cyberussell curriculum.

It is also the one that no other AI learning platform teaches.

Every other platform teaches software.

Cyberussell teaches collaboration.

The distinction is this: most people treat AI like a search engine. They type a question. They read an answer. They accept what they get or they try again with the same approach.

Think with AI teaches something entirely different — that working with AI is a skill, and that skill can be learned, practiced, and improved.

The learner who completes this Pillar does not just use AI better.

They think alongside AI.

They know how to frame a problem before asking for help. They know how to give AI what it needs to be useful. They know how to push back, iterate, and evaluate. They know when AI is doing their thinking for them — and they know how to take that thinking back.

This Pillar is Cyberussell's biggest differentiator.

It must be protected.

---

# Purpose Boundary

Think with AI is exclusively about the learner's side of the conversation.

It teaches how to structure input, how to evaluate output, how to iterate, and how to maintain judgment.

It does not teach tool-specific features.

It does not teach which AI to use for which task (that is Pillar 3).

It does not teach how tools work together (that is Pillar 4).

It does not teach domain skills like writing, coding, or design (that is Pillar 5).

What Think with AI teaches is transferable to every AI tool, every domain, and every task the learner will ever encounter — including AI tools that do not exist yet.

That transferability is the point.

# Transformation

## Who enters this Pillar

A learner entering Think with AI has completed AI Foundations.

They:

- Know what AI is and how it generates responses
- Have written at least one prompt using the four context elements
- Understand that AI fails in predictable ways
- Know hallucination is a real risk and can identify it
- Have a general sense of which AI to use for general tasks
- Have a personal privacy rule they apply when using AI

They are not yet confident working with AI independently.

They are still writing one prompt, reading the response, and either accepting it or starting over.

They have not yet learned to push back, build on responses, decompose complex problems, or maintain their own judgment when AI sounds authoritative.

## Who leaves this Pillar

A learner who completes Think with AI:

- Breaks complex problems into parts before prompting AI
- Gives AI examples of what they want — not just descriptions
- Assigns AI a role when doing so improves the output
- Continues a conversation with AI across multiple turns to refine results
- Knows how to iterate — what to change, how to ask for improvement, and when to stop
- Can evaluate an AI output against a specific standard and identify what is missing
- Maintains ownership of their own judgment — uses AI as input, not as authority
- Can use AI to explore options in a decision without letting AI make the decision for them

The transformation is from user to collaborator.

# Learning Outcomes

Upon completing all Guides in Think with AI, a learner will be able to:

1. Break any complex task into smaller sub-tasks before giving them to AI
2. Provide AI with a relevant example that improves the quality of the output
3. Assign AI a role that makes its response more appropriate to the situation
4. Conduct a multi-turn conversation with AI that builds toward a specific result
5. Apply a deliberate iteration strategy to improve an AI output that falls short
6. Evaluate an AI output against a specific quality standard and identify what is missing
7. Distinguish between AI input and their own judgment — and know which to act on
8. Use AI to generate and evaluate options for a real decision without outsourcing the decision itself

These eight outcomes map directly to the eight Guides in this Pillar.

# Scope Boundaries

## What Think with AI covers

- Problem decomposition — breaking complex tasks into prompts AI can handle
- Providing examples — showing AI what good looks like before asking for it
- Role assignment — giving AI a specific perspective or expertise
- Multi-turn conversation — building on previous responses instead of starting over
- Iteration strategy — how to refine an output that is close but not right
- Output evaluation — how to assess whether an AI response meets a specific standard
- Judgment ownership — how to stay in control of your own thinking
- Decision support — how to use AI to explore options without ceding the decision

## What Think with AI deliberately does not cover

**Belongs to AI Foundations (Pillar 1)**

- What AI is and how it generates responses
- The four context elements (task, context, format, constraints)
- Basic prompt construction from scratch
- Hallucination — why it happens and how to catch it
- General tool selection by task type
- Privacy and responsible use

**Belongs to Meet Your AI Team (Pillar 3)**

- Tool-specific prompting behavior (e.g., how Claude responds to role prompts differently than ChatGPT)
- Feature-specific interaction models (Memory, Projects, Canvas, Artifacts, Deep Research)
- Interface-level guidance of any kind

**Belongs to AI Workflows (Pillar 4)**

- Coordinating multiple AI tools
- Workflow design and sequencing
- Handoffs between AI tools

**Belongs to Build Real Skills (Pillar 5)**

- Domain-specific prompting strategies (writing prompts, coding prompts, SEO prompts)
- Skill-specific judgment frameworks

## Boundary note on context

AI Foundations Guide 6 taught one principle: adding context improves results. It introduced the four context elements as a beginner framework.

Think with AI goes deeper — not by repeating the four elements, but by teaching specific types of context that dramatically change what AI produces:

- Showing an example (Guide 2)
- Assigning a role (Guide 3)
- Continuing a conversation (Guide 4)

These are different skills, not extensions of the same lesson.

Guide writers must not re-explain the four context elements. Link to AI Foundations Guide 6 and proceed.

# Guide Objectives

## Guide 1 — How to Break Any Problem Down for AI

**Slug:** `break-problems-down`

**Difficulty:** Beginner

**Type:** Concept

**Time:** 5 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to divide any task into smaller parts before giving them to AI — so that each prompt has one job.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

AI produces better outputs when each prompt has a single, clear job. Complex tasks overwhelm a single prompt the same way a single worker overwhelmed with ten simultaneous instructions will drop some of them. Breaking a problem into parts means each part can be addressed well.

This is not about writing shorter prompts.

It is about thinking clearly before prompting at all.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Multi-turn conversation strategy (Guide 4), iteration (Guide 5), or any technique that builds on this one.

**Analogy:** Think of it like preparing a shopping list before going to the market. You do not walk in and say "give me food for the week." You list each item. Each item has one answer. Prompting AI without breaking the task down first is like walking into the market and hoping the vendor knows what your family eats.

**The decomposition rule:** Before writing any prompt for a complex task, ask: "What are the separate jobs inside this task?" Write each job as a separate prompt. Run them in order.

**The three signals that a task needs decomposition:**

1. The task requires more than one kind of output (e.g., research AND writing AND formatting)
2. The task involves more than one decision point
3. The learner cannot state in one sentence exactly what they want AI to produce

**Exercise:** Provide a complex task (e.g., "Help me prepare for a job interview"). Ask the learner to list the separate jobs inside it before running any prompt. Then provide a Tier 1 prompt for the first sub-task only and run it.

**Why this exercise:** The learning is in the listing, not the prompting. The moment the learner writes out the sub-tasks, they understand decomposition.

## Guide 2 — How to Show AI What You Mean

**Slug:** `show-ai-what-you-mean`

**Difficulty:** Beginner

**Type:** Skill

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to include a relevant example in your prompt that makes AI's response more accurate and more useful the first time.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

AI is better at matching a pattern it has been shown than at inferring a pattern from a description alone. Telling AI "write in a friendly tone" produces something generic. Showing AI a sentence written in that tone — and saying "write like this" — produces something specific. An example is the fastest way to communicate what you actually want.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Advanced few-shot prompting as a technical concept, role assignment (Guide 3), or anything beyond the single principle: showing beats describing.

**Distinction from AI Foundations:** AI Foundations Guide 6 taught that adding context improves results. This Guide teaches that an example is the most powerful form of context — more powerful than adjectives, more powerful than descriptions, more powerful than lengthy instructions.

**The two types of examples:**

1. **Output example** — "Write something like this: [paste example]"
2. **Standard example** — "This is what good looks like: [paste example]. Now do the same for my situation."

**When to use an example:**

- When the learner is asking for a specific tone, voice, or style
- When the learner has a format they want AI to follow
- When descriptions alone have not produced the right output

**Exercise — Tier 1 then Tier 2:**

Tier 1: Run a provided prompt without an example. Read the output.
Tier 2: Add an example to the same prompt. Run it again. Compare.

The comparison is the lesson. The learner will see the difference themselves.

**Why this exercise:** Showing the learner that one example changes the output more than a paragraph of description is more convincing than explaining why.

---

## Guide 3 — How to Give AI a Role

**Slug:** `give-ai-a-role`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Skill

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to assign AI a relevant role at the start of a prompt — and explain why the role you chose is appropriate for the task.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

When you tell AI to act as a specific type of person — a professional, an expert, a persona — it draws more selectively on the patterns in its training that match that role. A prompt that begins "You are a hiring manager reviewing resumes" produces a different perspective than a prompt that begins "Review this resume." The role focuses AI's response. It does not change what AI knows. It changes which part of what it knows it draws from.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Tool-specific behavior differences when using roles (Pillar 3), persona creation for marketing (Pillar 5), or system prompt design (too advanced for this Pillar).

**The role formula:** "You are a [specific role] with [relevant experience or context]. [Task]."

**Three rules for role assignment:**

1. The role must be relevant — assigning a role that has nothing to do with the task produces no benefit
2. The role must be specific — "expert" is too vague; "a Filipino HR manager who reviews resumes for remote work positions" is specific
3. The role should reflect the audience or perspective the learner needs, not just expertise

**When role assignment helps most:**

- Tasks that benefit from a specific professional perspective
- Tasks where tone and voice need to match a specific relationship (mentor, peer, critic)
- Tasks where the learner wants AI to notice things a particular type of person would notice

**When role assignment is unnecessary:**

- Simple, factual tasks
- Tasks where the learner wants a neutral, general response
- Tasks where the role would be so general it adds no focus

**Exercise — Tier 2:**

Provide a base prompt without a role. Ask the learner to add a specific role that is appropriate for the task. Run both versions and compare. Ask the learner to write one sentence explaining why they chose that role.

**Why this exercise:** The one-sentence explanation forces the learner to understand why the role works — not just copy a technique.

---

## Guide 4 — How to Build on What AI Gives You

**Slug:** `build-on-ai-responses`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Skill

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to use AI's previous response as the starting point for your next prompt — building toward a result across multiple turns instead of starting over each time.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

Most learners treat each AI interaction as a standalone event. They ask a question, read the answer, and either accept it or start an entirely new conversation. But AI can hold context within a single conversation. Each response can be the raw material for the next prompt. Building on what AI gives you — rather than starting fresh — produces better results faster and teaches the learner to think of AI as a collaborator in an ongoing exchange.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Iteration strategy for improving flawed outputs (Guide 5 — this Guide teaches building on good outputs, not fixing bad ones), or tool-specific memory features (Pillar 3).

**The distinction between Guide 4 and Guide 5:**

- Guide 4: AI gave a useful response. Build on it to go further.
- Guide 5: AI gave a flawed response. Iterate to improve it.

Both are multi-turn. The intent is different. Guide 4 is about extension. Guide 5 is about correction.

**The three multi-turn moves:**

1. **Expand** — "Take the second point you just made and go deeper."
2. **Apply** — "Now apply what you just wrote to [specific situation]."
3. **Transform** — "Rewrite what you just produced as [different format/tone/audience]."

**What the learner should do before continuing a conversation:**

Read the full response before the next prompt.
Identify one specific thing to build on — not the whole response.
Write the next prompt around that one thing.

**Exercise — Tier 1 then Tier 2:**

Tier 1: Run a provided starter prompt and read the full response.
Tier 2: Use one of the three multi-turn moves (expand, apply, or transform) to write the next prompt. Run it. Read how the conversation developed.

**Why this exercise:** Many learners will see for the first time that AI retains context and that conversation builds something better than a single prompt ever could.

---

## Guide 5 — How to Iterate Until You Get What You Need

**Slug:** `iterate-until-right`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Skill

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to apply a specific iteration strategy when an AI response falls short — changing one variable at a time until the output meets your standard.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

Most learners, when AI produces a disappointing response, either accept it or abandon the conversation and start over. Neither approach is correct. Iteration is the skill of identifying specifically what is wrong with an output and changing exactly that — one thing at a time — to get closer to the result needed.

Iteration is not trying harder.

Iteration is being more specific about what fell short.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Evaluation frameworks for assessing quality (Guide 6 — iterate assumes the learner can identify what fell short, but the evaluation skill itself belongs to Guide 6). Teach iteration first. Teach critique second. The order matters because critique requires more precision than iteration.

**The iteration framework — four moves:**

1. **Add specificity** — "Make the second paragraph more specific. Replace general claims with concrete examples."
2. **Change a variable** — "Rewrite this in a shorter format" or "Change the tone to be more direct."
3. **Constrain the scope** — "Focus only on [one part]. Remove everything else."
4. **Restart with better input** — "Ignore your previous response. Here is additional context: [new information]. Try again."

**The one-variable rule:**

Change one thing per iteration.

Changing multiple things at once makes it impossible to know what improved the output.

**When to stop iterating:**

Stop when the output meets the standard defined before the conversation began.

If no standard was defined, the learner will iterate forever.

Defining the standard before prompting is part of this skill.

**Exercise — Tier 2:**

Provide a prompt that reliably produces a mediocre response. Ask the learner to run it, identify specifically what fell short (one thing), apply one iteration move, and run again. Compare the two responses.

**Why this exercise:** The learner experiences that one targeted change produces more improvement than a complete restart. That experience changes how they approach AI permanently.

## Guide 6 — How to Critique What AI Gives You

**Slug:** `critique-ai-output`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Concept

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to evaluate any AI output against a specific standard — and name exactly what is missing, what is wrong, and what to do next.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

Iteration without evaluation is circular. A learner who knows how to iterate but not how to evaluate will change things without knowing whether they improved. This Guide teaches a structured evaluation habit: before accepting or revising any AI output, the learner applies a three-question test.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Domain-specific evaluation standards (Pillar 5 — this Guide teaches a general evaluation habit, not how to evaluate a resume or a cover letter specifically).

**Distinction from Guide 5:** Guide 5 teaches what to do when output falls short. Guide 6 teaches how to determine whether output falls short. The order is deliberate — most learners need to experience iteration before they understand why evaluation matters.

**The three-question evaluation test:**

1. **Does it do what I asked?**
Read what you asked. Read what AI produced. Does the output address the actual task — or did AI interpret the prompt differently than intended?

2. **Is it specific enough to use?**
A response full of general statements is not useful. Every claim, recommendation, and piece of advice should be specific enough to act on. If the learner cannot act on it without further clarification, it is not specific enough.

3. **Would I stand behind this?**
If the learner were to send this output to a client, employer, or colleague — would they be comfortable? If there is hesitation, the output is not complete.

**What happens after evaluation:**

- All three questions answered yes → accept or minor edit
- One question answered no → iterate with Guide 5 techniques
- Two or three questions answered no → the prompt likely needs redesigning, not refining

**Exercise — Critical Thinking, Tier 2:**

Provide an AI output alongside the prompt that produced it. Ask the learner to apply the three-question test to the output and write one sentence answer for each question. Then ask: "What would you do next?"

**Why this exercise:** Evaluation is a habit. The exercise builds the habit by making it explicit before making it automatic.

## Guide 7 — How to Keep the Thinking Yours

**Slug:** `keep-thinking-yours`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Concept

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to distinguish between using AI as input and using AI as authority — and apply one rule to stay in control of your own judgment.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

AI is persuasive. It writes confidently. It sounds authoritative. A learner who is not careful will find their own thinking replaced by AI's — accepting AI's framing, AI's conclusions, and AI's priorities without noticing. This is the most subtle risk in working with AI, and the hardest to detect.

The skill this Guide teaches is not skepticism.

It is authorship.

The learner who keeps the thinking theirs is not one who doubts everything AI says.

They are the one who uses AI's output as one input among several — and makes the final judgment themselves.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Ethics or societal concerns about AI (too broad), AI bias in a technical sense (too advanced), or advice that sounds paranoid about AI.

**The distinction between input and authority:**

AI as input: "AI suggested three approaches. Here is why I chose the second one."

AI as authority: "AI said the second approach is best, so that's what I'll do."

The difference is not the conclusion. It is who made the decision.

**The two signs that AI has replaced your thinking:**

1. You cannot explain why you accepted an AI output — you just did.
2. You feel uncertain about a conclusion but follow it because AI was confident.

**The one rule:** Before using any AI output, complete this sentence in your own words: "I'm using this because..."

If the learner cannot complete that sentence, they are not using AI as input. They are using it as authority.

**Exercise — Reflection Activity:**

Provide an AI output on a topic the learner could have an opinion about (a career choice, a business decision, a skill recommendation). Ask: "Read this response. Does it match your own view? If it does, why? If it does not, what would you add or change?" Ask the learner to write their own position in two sentences — without using AI.

**Why this exercise:** Writing their own position after reading AI's forces the learner to locate their own judgment in relation to AI's output. Many will discover they agree with AI without having thought about it. The exercise makes that visible.

## Guide 8 — How to Use AI to Make Better Decisions

**Slug:** `use-ai-to-decide`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Applied

**Time:** 15 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to use AI to explore options for a real decision — generating perspectives, stress-testing your reasoning, and identifying blind spots — without letting AI make the decision for you.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

Decision-making is where the Think with AI toolkit pays off most directly. A learner who can break a decision into parts, assign AI a relevant perspective, build on responses across turns, iterate toward clarity, evaluate the output, and protect their own judgment is equipped to use AI as a genuine thinking partner.

This Guide is applied — it does not introduce new concepts. It integrates all seven previous Guides into a single decision-making framework.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Any new technique not introduced in Guides 1–7.

**The AI-assisted decision process:**

1. **State the decision** — Name it in one sentence. (Guide 1: decompose the decision from everything surrounding it.)
2. **Generate options** — Ask AI to list the strongest options for your situation. (Guides 2–3: use examples and roles to get relevant options.)
3. **Stress-test each option** — Ask AI to argue against each option. (Guide 4: build on the previous response.)
4. **Identify blind spots** — Ask AI what you may not have considered. (Guide 5: iterate toward completeness.)
5. **Evaluate AI's contributions** — Apply the three-question test to what AI produced. (Guide 6.)
6. **Make the decision** — In your own words, state the choice and why. AI does not make this step. (Guide 7.)

**What this process is not:**

It is not a formula to follow every time.

It is a demonstration that the Think with AI skills work together as a system.

The learner applies it once in the Exercise, then adapts it to their own decision-making style.

**Exercise — Tier 2 with optional Tier 3:**

Provide a real-life decision scenario the learner is likely to face (whether to offer a service at a lower price to get a first client, which skill to learn next, whether to apply for a remote job or start freelancing). Walk through the six-step process with provided Tier 1 and Tier 2 prompts. Optional Tier 3: the learner runs the process for an actual decision they are currently facing.

**Why this exercise:** This is the capstone exercise for the Pillar. It integrates everything. The learner who runs the optional Tier 3 version will have used AI to think through something that matters to them. That is the most powerful learning experience in this Pillar.

# Interactive Exercises

## Exercise Design by Guide

### Guide 1 — Hands-On Activity (Tier 1)

The learner is given a complex task and asked to list its sub-tasks before prompting. Then they run one Tier 1 prompt for the first sub-task only.

**Learning through:** The act of listing sub-tasks makes decomposition concrete.

### Guide 2 — Prompt Practice (Tier 1 → Tier 2)

The learner runs a prompt without an example, then adds an example and runs it again. They compare the two outputs.

**Learning through:** Direct comparison. The difference is visible and memorable.

### Guide 3 — Prompt Practice (Tier 2)

The learner adds a specific role to a base prompt, writes one sentence explaining the role choice, and runs both versions for comparison.

**Learning through:** Writing the justification builds understanding, not just imitation.

### Guide 4 — Hands-On Activity (Tier 1 → Tier 2)

The learner runs a starter prompt, reads the full response, then applies one of the three multi-turn moves (expand, apply, transform) to continue the conversation.

**Learning through:** Experiencing that a conversation produces something a single prompt cannot.

### Guide 5 — Revision Activity (Tier 2)

The learner runs a prompt that produces a mediocre response, names one specific flaw, applies one iteration move, and compares the result.

**Learning through:** The one-variable constraint makes the improvement traceable.

### Guide 6 — Critical Thinking Activity (Tier 2)

The learner evaluates a provided AI output using the three-question test and writes one-sentence answers for each question.

**Learning through:** Writing explicit answers builds the evaluation habit.

### Guide 7 — Reflection Activity

The learner reads an AI output on a real topic, writes their own two-sentence position, and identifies whether their position was influenced by AI or independent.

**Learning through:** The gap (or lack of gap) between AI's output and their own position makes judgment ownership visible.

### Guide 8 — Hands-On + Experimentation (Tier 2 + optional Tier 3)

The learner walks through the six-step AI-assisted decision process with provided prompts. Optional: they run the same process for a real decision they are currently facing.

# Mission

## Mission: Solve a Real Problem with AI

**Slug:** `solve-a-real-problem`

**Type:** Single-Tool

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Time:** 60 minutes

**Required Guides:** All eight Think with AI Guides (hard prerequisite), AI Foundations completion (hard prerequisite)

**Required AI:** One AI tool of the learner's choice

**Optional Tool:** Cyberussell Prompt Trainer (if available)

**Deliverable:** A documented AI-assisted problem-solving session — a written record of the real problem the learner worked through, the prompts they used, the outputs they got, the choices they made, and the conclusion they reached.

### Why This Deliverable

The deliverable for this Mission is not a resume, a business plan, or a piece of content.

Those belong to later Missions in Pillars 5 and 6.

The deliverable here is a documented thinking process.

The learner picks a real problem they are currently facing — a decision, a task, a challenge — and works through it using every skill they learned in this Pillar.

The document they produce is evidence of how they think with AI. It shows a future employer, client, or collaborator not just that they can use AI — but that they use AI deliberately.

### Deliverable Checklist

The deliverable is complete when it contains:

- [ ] A one-sentence statement of the problem or decision
- [ ] A list of the sub-tasks the learner identified before prompting (Guide 1)
- [ ] At least one prompt that includes an example (Guide 2)
- [ ] At least one prompt with a role assignment (Guide 3)
- [ ] At least three consecutive turns in the same conversation (Guide 4)
- [ ] Evidence of at least one iteration — original prompt plus improved version (Guide 5)
- [ ] A three-question evaluation of at least one AI output (Guide 6)
- [ ] A final conclusion written entirely by the learner — no AI output (Guides 7–8)

### Mission Steps

**Step 1 — Choose the problem (5 min)**

The learner identifies a real problem or decision they are currently facing. It must be real. It must be specific. It cannot be hypothetical.

Examples: Should I apply for this remote job or keep freelancing? What skill should I learn next? How do I write a message to my first potential client?

They write the problem in one sentence.

**Step 2 — Decompose (5 min)**

Before any prompting, the learner lists the sub-tasks inside the problem. What are the separate questions this problem contains? They write the list.

**Step 3 — First prompt with example and role (10 min)**

The learner writes a prompt for the first sub-task. The prompt must include a relevant example and a role assignment. They run it and read the response.

**Step 4 — Build the conversation (15 min)**

The learner continues the conversation for at least two more turns, using the expand, apply, or transform moves from Guide 4. They document each prompt and a one-sentence summary of each response.

**Step 5 — Iterate on the weakest output (10 min)**

The learner identifies the output they found least useful and applies one iteration move. They document the original prompt, what fell short, what they changed, and the new output.

**Step 6 — Evaluate and conclude (15 min)**

The learner applies the three-question evaluation test to one key output. Then they write their conclusion — the answer to the problem or the decision they have made — in their own words with no AI involvement. The conclusion must state why, in the learner's own judgment, they reached that answer.

### Mission Reflection Questions

1. Which Think with AI skill made the biggest difference to the quality of the conversation?
2. At what point, if any, did AI say something that changed how you were thinking about the problem?
3. Look at your final conclusion. How much of it came from AI — and how much came from you?
4. How would this conversation have gone if you had typed one single prompt instead of working through it step by step?

# Overlap Review

## Resolved: Context vs. Examples vs. Role

**Risk:** Guides 2 and 3 could be seen as repeating the context principle from AI Foundations Guide 6.

**Resolution:** AI Foundations teaches that context improves results. Guides 2 and 3 teach specific high-leverage types of context that produce disproportionately large improvements: examples and roles. These are skills, not repetitions. The key guard is that Guide writers must link to AI Foundations Guide 6 for the base principle and build from there without re-explaining it.

## Resolved: Iteration vs. Revision

**Risk:** Guide 5 (Iterate) and Guide 6 (Critique) could seem like the same skill.

**Resolution:** Iteration is the action — what to change and how. Critique is the evaluation — deciding whether change is needed and naming what specifically fell short. They are deliberately sequenced: the learner iterates in Guide 5 before they have a formal evaluation framework, which mirrors how most learners actually work. Guide 6 then gives the framework that makes future iteration more precise. Teaching critique before iteration would feel abstract. Teaching iteration before critique gives the learner something to improve.

## Resolved: Judgment Ownership vs. Responsible Use

**Risk:** Guide 7 (Keep the Thinking Yours) could drift toward AI ethics or societal concerns that belong in a different context.

**Resolution:** Guide 7 teaches one concrete, personal skill: distinguishing between AI as input and AI as authority. Ethics and societal impact are important but outside the scope of this Guide. The test is: "Can the learner apply this in their next AI interaction?" If the concept requires societal context to be actionable, it does not belong in this Guide.

## Resolved: Decision Support vs. AI Workflows

**Risk:** Guide 8's six-step AI-assisted decision process could look like a workflow — and overlap with Pillar 4.

**Resolution:** Guide 8's process is a single-tool, single-conversation framework for a personal decision. Pillar 4 teaches multi-tool workflows for professional projects. The distinction is: Guide 8 is about how to think, Pillar 4 is about how to build. Guide 8 must not introduce multiple AI tools, must not sequence tools across steps, and must not use project or workflow language. One conversation. One tool. One decision.

## Resolved: Think with AI exercises vs. Meet Your AI Team examples

**Risk:** Exercises in this Pillar could use tool-specific examples (e.g., "Run this in Claude") in ways that pre-empt Pillar 3.

**Resolution:** Think with AI exercises specify "run this in your preferred AI tool" or "run this in ChatGPT" only when the task is broadly appropriate for all tools. No exercise may reference a tool-specific feature. No exercise may compare tools by name as part of the exercise logic (that belongs to Comparison Exercises in Pillar 3). The Comparison Exercise in Guide 2 compares two versions of a prompt — not two tools.

# Notes for Guide Writers

1. The word "collaborate" is the operating metaphor for this entire Pillar. Every Guide should reinforce that the learner is working with AI — not directing it, not accepting it, not fighting it.

2. Analogies must come from Filipino everyday life. Suggested starting points: working with a colleague on a project, commissioning work from a service provider, supervising an assistant, getting advice from a friend who is not an expert in your field.

3. Never let a Guide in this Pillar become a prompt engineering tutorial. Prompt engineering is a technical discipline with its own vocabulary. Think with AI teaches judgment skills that happen to be expressed through prompts. The difference matters in how the Guide is written.

4. The tone across this Pillar should shift subtly from Guide 1 to Guide 8 — from "here is what to do" (Beginner) to "here is how to think" (Intermediate). The vocabulary can stay simple. The implied expectations rise.

5. Guide 7 is the most important Guide in the Pillar from a long-term impact perspective. A learner who internalizes Guide 7 will use AI better for the rest of their life than one who skips it. Write it with that weight.

6. Guide 8 should feel like a graduation. It is the application of everything. The optional Tier 3 experience — working through a real personal decision — is the best learning experience in this Pillar. Make it easy to start and hard to skip.
