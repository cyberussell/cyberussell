# Purpose

AI Foundations is the first Pillar every Cyberussell learner enters.

Its purpose is not to teach AI.

Its purpose is to remove fear, install curiosity, and establish the mindset a learner needs to work with AI effectively for the rest of their life — regardless of which tools exist in the future.

A learner who completes AI Foundations does not know more features.

They think differently about what AI is, what it can do, what it cannot do, and how to interact with it honestly.

This Pillar teaches the permanent things: principles, judgment, and mental models.

It deliberately avoids everything that changes: interfaces, buttons, pricing, product names, and version-specific features.

Those belong to the Meet Your AI Team Pillar.

This Pillar builds the foundation that makes every other Pillar easier.

# Transformation

## Who enters this Pillar

A learner who enters AI Foundations may:

- Have never used an AI tool in their life
- Have used ChatGPT once or twice but not understood what they were doing
- Feel uncertain whether AI is "for them"
- Worry about doing something wrong
- Believe AI is only for programmers or educated professionals
- Have heard conflicting things about whether AI will take their job
- Be curious but overwhelmed by where to start

They do not know what a prompt is.

They do not know what a model is.

They do not know why AI sometimes sounds confident and is wrong.

They may believe AI thinks like a person.

They may believe AI knows everything.

They may believe AI is dangerous in ways that prevent them from touching it.

## Who leaves this Pillar

A learner who completes AI Foundations:

- Understands what AI is and what it actually does — in plain terms
- Has used at least one AI tool to produce something useful
- Knows the difference between what AI is good at and where it fails
- Knows why AI makes things up and how to catch it
- Can identify the right AI tool for a general task
- Understands why giving AI context produces better results
- Has made an active, informed choice to continue learning
- Trusts themselves to interact with AI without fear of doing it wrong

The transformation is from uncertainty to informed confidence.

Not expertise.

Confidence.

# Learning Outcomes

Upon completing all Guides in AI Foundations, a learner will be able to:

1. Explain in plain language what AI is and what it actually does
2. Distinguish between AI's real capabilities and common misconceptions
3. Recognize when an AI response may be hallucinated or unreliable
4. Identify the appropriate AI tool for a general task without external guidance
5. Write a basic prompt that includes enough context for AI to respond usefully
6. Make an informed decision about when to trust an AI output and when to verify it
7. Apply one responsible use principle when sharing information with an AI tool

These seven outcomes map directly to the seven Guides in this Pillar.

Each Guide produces exactly one outcome.

# Scope Boundaries

## What AI Foundations covers

- What AI is, at a conceptual level
- How language models work, in plain analogy (not technical depth)
- What AI does well
- What AI fails at
- Why hallucinations happen and how to detect them
- How to choose between AI tools based on task type — general rules only
- Responsible and safe use — what not to share, what to verify
- How to write a basic, usable prompt

## What AI Foundations deliberately does not cover

The following topics belong to later Pillars and must not appear in AI Foundations Guides.

**Belongs to Think with AI (Pillar 2)**

- Prompting techniques and frameworks
- Iteration strategies
- How to critique and improve AI outputs
- Problem decomposition
- Giving AI a role
- Multi-turn conversation strategy

**Belongs to Meet Your AI Team (Pillar 3)**

- ChatGPT-specific features (Memory, Projects, Voice, Canvas, Deep Research, Image Generation)
- Claude-specific features (Artifacts, Projects, long-context use, Claude Code)
- Gemini-specific features (Google Workspace integration, Search, Gmail)
- Comparing tools by feature set
- Tool-specific interface navigation of any kind

**Belongs to AI Workflows (Pillar 4)**

- Using multiple AI tools together
- Workflow design
- Tool chaining

**Belongs to Build Real Skills (Pillar 5)**

- Domain-specific AI use (writing, coding, design, SEO, etc.)

Any content that drifts into these areas during Guide writing must be flagged, cut, and replaced with a link to the appropriate Pillar.

The Guides must be completed in this order.

The order is not arbitrary.

Each Guide builds a mental model that the next Guide depends on.

```
Guide 1 — What AI Actually Is
  ↓
  Learner understands AI is a pattern-matcher, not a mind.
  
Guide 2 — What AI Is Good At
  ↓
  Learner understands AI's strengths come from pattern recognition.
  
Guide 3 — Where AI Fails
  ↓
  Learner understands AI's failures also come from pattern recognition.
  (This Guide requires Guide 2 — the contrast is essential.)
  
Guide 4 — Why AI Makes Things Up
```

# Guide Objectives

## Guide 1 — What AI Actually Is

**Slug:** `what-ai-actually-is`

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to explain in plain language what AI is and what it actually does — without using the words "smart," "thinks," or "knows."

**Core Concept to Teach:**

AI is a system trained on enormous amounts of human-written text. It learned to predict what words, sentences, and ideas follow other words, sentences, and ideas. It does not understand meaning the way a person does. It produces outputs that look like understanding because it was trained on the outputs of people who did understand.

This single idea — AI as pattern recognition, not thinking — must be the only concept in this Guide.

**What this Guide must not teach:** AI history, technical architecture, machine learning terminology, specific tools by name, or comparisons between AI systems.

**Analogy to use:** A learner who watched thousands of cooking shows and can describe how to cook a dish they have never tasted. The description may be accurate. The understanding is different from a chef's.

**Exercise:** Ask the learner to copy a prompt asking AI to explain something simple, then read the response and identify one thing AI said confidently that they would want to double-check.

**Why this exercise:** It immediately demonstrates the gap between AI's confident delivery and the learner's healthy skepticism — establishing the critical relationship from the very first Guide.

---

## Guide 2 — What AI Is Good At

**Slug:** `what-ai-is-good-at`

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to identify five types of tasks where AI consistently produces useful results — and explain why it works well for each one.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

AI is strong at tasks that benefit from the patterns inside large amounts of human language: summarizing, drafting, explaining, brainstorming, translating, formatting, and generating variations. These tasks share one quality: they do not require the AI to know something factually new. They require it to rearrange and express what is already known in language.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Specific AI tools by name, feature lists, which tool is best for each task, or anything that belongs to Meet Your AI Team.

**The five strength categories:**

1. Drafting and writing — producing a first version of almost anything
2. Summarizing — condensing long content into shorter content
3. Explaining — restating complex ideas in simpler terms
4. Brainstorming — generating options, ideas, and variations
5. Organizing and formatting — taking unstructured input and giving it structure

**Exercise:** Ask the learner to pick one task from the five categories that is relevant to their own life or work goal, then run a provided Tier 1 prompt for that task and evaluate whether the output was useful.

**Why this exercise:** The learner immediately connects AI's strengths to their own context — not to an abstract list.

---

## Guide 3 — Where AI Fails

**Slug:** `where-ai-fails`

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to identify three situations where AI is likely to produce a poor result — before you run the prompt.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

AI fails predictably, not randomly. The same properties that make it strong at drafting and explaining make it weak at tasks that require real-time facts, precise numerical reasoning, personal knowledge, or deep domain accuracy. Understanding why AI fails is more useful than knowing that it fails — because it teaches the learner where not to rely on it.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Hallucination (Guide 4 covers this), specific tool failures, or anything that could be interpreted as a tool comparison.

**The three failure categories:**

1. Current facts — AI's training has a cutoff. It does not know what happened recently.
2. Personal or private information — AI has no knowledge of the learner's specific situation unless they provide it.
3. Precise technical accuracy — In specialized domains (medicine, law, finance, engineering), AI's confident output should always be verified by a qualified human.

**What this Guide must not do:** Make the learner afraid of AI. The frame is: "Every tool has its limits. Knowing the limits makes you a better user."

**Exercise:** Provide three prompts — one that falls into each failure category. Ask the learner to run them and identify which response they would trust without verification and which they would not.

**Why this exercise:** Real failure responses are more convincing than descriptions of failure. Seeing a confident but wrong AI answer is the most memorable lesson in this Guide.

---

## Guide 4 — Why AI Makes Things Up

**Slug:** `why-ai-makes-things-up`

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to identify when an AI response may be hallucinated and know what to do next.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

Hallucination is not a bug. It is a natural result of how AI works. Because AI was trained to produce plausible-sounding output, it will produce plausible-sounding output even when the factual answer is not in its training data. It fills the gap with what sounds right. It does not know it is wrong. It has no mechanism for knowing it is wrong.

This is the single most important concept in AI Foundations.

A learner who understands hallucination will never fully trust an AI output on a consequential claim without verifying it.

**What this Guide must not teach:** How to write better prompts to reduce hallucinations (Guide 6 covers prompting), specific tool differences in hallucination rates (Pillar 3), or technical explanations of model architecture.

**Hallucination patterns to teach:**

1. Fake citations — AI invents books, studies, authors, and URLs that sound real
2. Confident wrong numbers — statistics, dates, prices, and figures that are plausible but incorrect
3. Invented details — filling in gaps in a story, biography, or description with fabricated specifics

**Detection method to teach:**

One rule: if an AI output contains a specific claim you would quote, cite, or act on, verify it from a non-AI source before using it.

**Exercise:** Provide a prompt that reliably triggers hallucination (asking for a specific statistic, a book citation, or a named expert's quote). Ask the learner to run it, then verify one claim from the response. Compare what AI said to what they find.

**Why this exercise:** The learner personally discovers a hallucination. That discovery is more memorable than any explanation.

---

## Guide 5 — How to Choose the Right AI

**Slug:** `how-to-choose-the-right-ai`

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to choose between ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini for a general task based on what each AI is known for — without needing to test all three every time.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

Not all AI tools are equal for every task. Each major tool has a profile: what it does well, what it does less well, and what kind of tasks benefit from its specific strengths. Choosing the right tool is a judgment skill, not a rule to memorize. This Guide teaches the general profile — not the features. Feature-specific knowledge belongs in Pillar 3.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Specific features (Memory, Artifacts, Deep Research, Workspace integration), pricing, interface details, account types, or any recommendation to subscribe to a paid tier.

**The three general profiles — at a concept level only:**

| AI | General Strength Profile |
|---|---|
| ChatGPT | Broad capability across many task types; strong for general-purpose thinking, planning, and creative work |
| Claude | Strong for writing quality, reasoning through complex problems, and working with long documents |
| Gemini | Strong for tasks connected to real-time information, current events, and Google products |

The critical teaching point: these profiles are general. Every task deserves a judgment call. This Guide teaches the learner to make that call — not to follow a fixed rule.

**What this Guide must not do:** Rank tools against each other or imply one is objectively better. The right tool depends on the task and the person using it.

**Exercise:** Provide three tasks. Ask the learner to decide which AI to use for each one, write one sentence explaining why, then run one of the three in their chosen AI. No wrong answers — the judgment is the exercise.

**Why this exercise:** The learner practices making a reasoned choice rather than following a prescribed answer.

---

## Guide 6 — Your First Real Prompt

**Slug:** `your-first-real-prompt`

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to write a prompt that includes enough context for AI to respond usefully the first time — for any task connected to your own life or work.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

A prompt is the instruction you give AI. The quality of the prompt determines the quality of the response. AI cannot read your mind. It does not know your situation. It does not know your audience, your goal, your tone preference, or your constraints. When you give AI more context, it produces more relevant output.

This is a Skill Guide — the only one in this Pillar.

It applies everything the learner has understood in Guides 1–5 into a single practical act: writing a useful prompt.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Advanced prompting techniques (role assignment, chain-of-thought, multi-step iteration, prompt engineering frameworks). All of those belong to Pillar 2. This Guide teaches one thing: context improves results.

**The four context elements to teach:**

1. **Task** — What do you want AI to do?
2. **Context** — What does AI need to know about your situation?
3. **Format** — How do you want the response structured?
4. **Constraints** — What should AI avoid or limit?

These four elements form the learner's first prompt framework.

They are not a formula.

They are a checklist the learner uses until writing good prompts becomes intuitive.

**What this Guide must not do:** Introduce the word "framework" as a technical term, or imply that prompts following this structure are always better than simpler prompts. Simplicity is sometimes correct.

**Exercise:** Ask the learner to write a prompt about a real task in their own life using the four context elements. Provide a Tier 1 example prompt and a Tier 2 modification challenge. The learner runs their own prompt and compares the result to what a context-free version would have produced.

**Why this exercise:** The learner writes their first intentional prompt. It is theirs. It is for a real task. The result belongs to them.

---

## Guide 7 — What Not to Share with AI

**Slug:** `what-not-to-share-with-ai`

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to apply one practical rule for protecting your personal and professional information when using any AI tool.

**Core Concept to Teach:**

When you type something into an AI tool, that input may be used to improve the model. It may be stored. It may be reviewed by humans at the company. It is not a private conversation. This does not mean AI is dangerous. It means it requires the same judgment as any digital tool: do not share information you would not want seen by a stranger.

**Why this Guide is last:** A learner who has never used AI does not have meaningful privacy concerns because they have nothing to share yet. Placing this Guide after Guide 6 — when the learner has just written their first real prompt — makes the lesson immediately applicable to something they just did.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Technical explanations of data storage, legal frameworks, or specific company privacy policies (those change). Teach the principle, not the policy.

**The one rule to teach:**

Before sharing any information with an AI tool, ask: "Would I be comfortable if this were public?"

If yes, proceed.

If no, remove or generalize it before typing.

**The four categories of information to protect:**

1. Full name combined with other personal details (address, phone, ID numbers)
2. Financial information (bank details, credit card numbers, salary specifics)
3. Client or employer information (company data, client names, internal documents)
4. Health information (medical history, diagnoses, medications)

**What this Guide must not do:** Create fear or imply that AI tools are unsafe. The tone is: this is a tool. Like any tool, knowing how to use it safely is part of using it well.

**Exercise:** A Reflection Activity. Ask the learner to think back to the prompt they wrote in Guide 6. Ask: "Would any part of that prompt make you uncomfortable if it were made public?" If yes, ask what they would change. This exercise requires no new AI interaction — it applies the principle to something the learner has already done.

**Why this exercise:** It closes the loop on the entire Pillar. The learner reviews their own first prompt through the lens of responsible use.

# Interactive Exercises

## Exercise Design by Guide

### Guide 1 — Hands-On Activity (Tier 1)

The learner copies a prompt asking AI to explain a familiar concept, then reads the response and identifies one claim they would want to verify.

Purpose: Establish healthy skepticism from the first interaction.

### Guide 2 — Hands-On Activity (Tier 1)

The learner picks one of the five strength categories relevant to their own situation, runs a provided prompt, and evaluates whether the output was useful for their actual needs.

Purpose: Connect AI's strengths to the learner's personal context.

### Guide 3 — Critical Thinking Activity (Tier 1)

The learner runs three provided prompts — one in each failure category — and identifies which responses they would trust and which they would not.

Purpose: The learner personally encounters AI failure. Seeing is more convincing than reading.

### Guide 4 — Critical Thinking Activity (Tier 1)

The learner runs a prompt designed to produce a hallucinated citation or statistic, then verifies one claim from the response using any non-AI source.

Purpose: The learner discovers a hallucination themselves. The memory is permanent.

### Guide 5 — Comparison Activity (Tier 1)

The learner is given three tasks and decides which AI to use for each, writes one sentence of reasoning, then runs one task in their chosen AI.

Purpose: The learner practices judgment — choosing rather than following instructions.

### Guide 6 — Prompt Practice Activity (Tier 1 + optional Tier 2)

The learner writes a prompt about a real task using the four context elements (Tier 1 example provided). Optional Tier 2: rewrite a context-free version of the same prompt and compare results.

Purpose: The learner writes their first intentional, contextual prompt for a real need.

### Guide 7 — Reflection Activity

The learner reviews the prompt they wrote in Guide 6 and identifies whether any part of it would make them uncomfortable if public — and what they would change.

No new AI interaction required.

Purpose: Close the loop on responsible use applied to something the learner actually did.

## Exercise Progression Across the Pillar

```
Guide 1    Hands-On        → Encounter AI, build healthy skepticism
Guide 2    Hands-On        → Connect AI strengths to personal context
Guide 3    Critical Think  → Witness AI failure
Guide 4    Critical Think  → Discover hallucination personally
Guide 5    Comparison      → Practice judgment (choose, don't follow)
Guide 6    Prompt Practice → Write the first intentional prompt
Guide 7    Reflection      → Apply responsible use to own work
```

The exercises move from passive encounter → active judgment → first creation → responsible awareness.

This is the full arc of the Pillar in seven steps.

# Mission

## Mission: Know Your AI

**Slug:** `know-your-ai`

**Type:** Single-Tool

**Difficulty:** Beginner

**Time:** 45 minutes

**Required Guides:** All seven Guides in AI Foundations (hard prerequisite)

**Required AI:** Any one AI tool of the learner's choice

**Deliverable:** A personal AI profile — a one-page document the learner writes that describes how they plan to use AI based on what they learned in this Pillar.

### Why This Deliverable

A resume, a landing page, or a proposal would be premature here.

The learner has just encountered AI for the first time with intention.

The most appropriate deliverable at this stage is a document that requires the learner to synthesize what they learned and make it personal — to turn abstract knowledge into a personal plan.

This deliverable is also reusable: the learner can update it as they progress through the curriculum, making it a living record of how their relationship with AI evolves.

### Deliverable Checklist

The deliverable is complete when it contains:

- [ ] One paragraph explaining what AI is in the learner's own words (no jargon)
- [ ] A list of three tasks where the learner would use AI in their own life or work
- [ ] A list of two situations where the learner would not trust AI without verifying
- [ ] One sentence naming the AI tool the learner plans to use most and why
- [ ] One privacy rule the learner will apply every time they use AI

### Mission Steps

**Step 1** — Answer: What is AI to me? (10 min)

The learner uses AI to brainstorm how they would explain AI in their own words. They then write the explanation themselves, using the AI-generated ideas as a starting point — not as the final answer.

**Step 2** — Identify: Where will I use AI? (10 min)

The learner describes three real tasks from their own work, study, or daily life where AI could help. For each task, they write one sentence about what they expect AI to do.

**Step 3** — Identify: Where will I verify? (10 min)

The learner names two situations from their own context where they would not use AI output without checking it first. For each, they write one sentence explaining why.

**Step 4** — Choose: My primary AI tool (5 min)

The learner names the AI tool they plan to use most often and writes one sentence explaining the choice based on what they learned in Guide 5.

**Step 5** — Commit: My privacy rule (10 min)

The learner writes one personal privacy rule — in their own words, specific to their situation — that they will apply every time they use AI.

### Mission Reflection Questions

1. What surprised you most about what you learned in AI Foundations?
2. What is one thing you believed about AI before this Pillar that you now think differently about?
3. Looking at your personal AI profile — which of the three tasks you listed are you most excited to try with AI?

# Overlap Review

The following potential overlaps were identified and resolved during curriculum design.

## Resolved: Prompting vs. Think with AI

**Risk:** Guide 6 (Your First Real Prompt) could drift into advanced prompting territory that belongs in Pillar 2.

**Resolution:** Guide 6 teaches exactly one prompting principle: context improves results. The four context elements (task, context, format, constraints) are a beginner framework, not a prompting system. All strategies — iteration, role assignment, chain of thought — are explicitly deferred to Pillar 2 and must not appear in Guide 6 writing.

## Resolved: Tool profiles vs. Meet Your AI Team

**Risk:** Guide 5 (How to Choose the Right AI) could be expanded into a full tool comparison that duplicates Pillar 3.

**Resolution:** Guide 5 teaches general profiles at a conceptual level only. No feature names. No interface details. The moment a guide writer types the name of a specific feature (Memory, Artifacts, Deep Research), that content must move to Pillar 3.

## Resolved: Privacy vs. Responsible AI (future content)

**Risk:** Guide 7 (What Not to Share with AI) could expand into a full AI ethics curriculum — bias, environmental impact, labor displacement — that is beyond the scope of this Pillar.

**Resolution:** Guide 7 teaches exactly one principle: personal and professional information requires judgment before sharing. Ethics, bias, and societal impact are important topics — and they belong in a future AI Foundations expansion, not in the current Beginner Guide.

## Resolved: Hallucination detection vs. Critiquing AI (Pillar 2)

**Risk:** Guide 4 (Why AI Makes Things Up) could expand into a full framework for evaluating AI outputs — which belongs in Think with AI.

**Resolution:** Guide 4 teaches why hallucination happens and one detection rule: verify specific claims before using them. The skill of systematically evaluating and critiquing AI outputs is Pillar 2 territory and must not appear here.

## Resolved: Mission scope vs. future Missions

**Risk:** The Know Your AI Mission could try to do too much — include a resume, a profile, and a plan — and compete with future Beginner Missions in Pillar 6.

**Resolution:** The Mission produces exactly one deliverable: a personal AI profile. It is the right size for a learner who has just completed seven Concept Guides at Beginner level. It does not produce a professional output — it produces a personal one. Professional outputs begin in Pillar 5 and Pillar 6.

# Notes for Guide Writers

1. Every analogy in this Pillar must be grounded in Filipino everyday life. See Guide Canon analogy standards.

2. Never use the word "smart" to describe AI. AI is not smart. It is trained. The distinction matters.

3. Never use the word "thinks" to describe what AI does. AI does not think. It generates. The distinction is the core concept of Guide 1 and must be consistent across all seven Guides.

4. Avoid the word "algorithm" unless it is immediately explained in plain language. Prefer: "a set of patterns" or "a system trained on text."

5. Every Guide must end with a clear next step. The transition from Guide 7 to the Mission — and from the Mission to Pillar 2 — must feel earned, not arbitrary.

6. The tone throughout this Pillar is: curious, grounded, and honest. Not evangelistic about AI. Not fearful about AI. Honest about what it is and what it is not.
