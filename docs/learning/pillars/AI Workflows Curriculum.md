# Purpose

Most people who use AI do so one prompt at a time.

They open a tool. They type. They read. They close the tab.

A few minutes later they open a different tool. They type again. They read again.

These interactions are not connected. They do not build toward anything. Each one is an isolated event.

AI Workflows teaches something fundamentally different: that real work — professional work, the kind that earns money and builds a reputation — is not a single prompt. It is a sequence. It is a process. It is a set of deliberate steps that use the right tool at the right moment to produce something better than any single interaction could.

This Pillar teaches learners to design and run those sequences.

Not abstractly.

Not theoretically.

In direct service of real problems they face: researching an opportunity, writing a proposal, building a portfolio piece, planning a project, creating content that gets results.

A learner who completes AI Workflows does not just use AI more.

They use it in a completely different way — as a system, not a shortcut.

# Purpose Boundary

AI Workflows teaches the design and execution of multi-tool, multi-step AI processes oriented toward a practical outcome.

## What this Pillar covers

- What a workflow is and why single-prompt thinking fails for complex tasks
- How to identify the phases of a real task and assign the right AI to each
- How to pass output from one tool into the next without losing quality
- Specific named workflows for tasks Filipino freelancers, remote workers, and creators actually do
- How to evaluate the output of a workflow — not just the output of a single prompt
- How to improve a workflow when results fall short
- How to build reusable workflow templates for work you do repeatedly

## What this Pillar deliberately does not cover

**Belongs to AI Foundations (Pillar 1)**

- What AI is and how it generates responses
- Hallucination and why AI makes things up
- Privacy and responsible use

**Belongs to Think with AI (Pillar 2)**

- How to write better individual prompts
- Iteration and evaluation at the single-prompt level
- Maintaining judgment when AI sounds authoritative

**Belongs to Meet Your AI Team (Pillar 3)**

- Which AI tool has which strengths
- How to choose the right AI for a given task
- Tool-specific behavioral character

**Belongs to Build Real Skills (Pillar 5)**

- Domain-specific skills: how to write well, how to code, how to design
- Skills that require human expertise to develop — AI assists, but the skill belongs to the learner

## The critical distinction between Pillar 3 and Pillar 4

Pillar 3 teaches tool selection: before I start, which AI do I use?

Pillar 4 teaches workflow design: how do I sequence multiple tools across multiple steps to produce a professional result?

Pillar 3 is a decision before starting.

Pillar 4 is a system for doing.

A learner who knows which tool to use (Pillar 3) and how to collaborate with it (Pillar 2) is ready to learn how to chain those tools into a workflow that produces something real (Pillar 4).

# Transformation

## Who enters this Pillar

A learner entering AI Workflows has completed AI Foundations and Meet Your AI Team.

They can choose the right AI for a given task. They can write a clear prompt. They can iterate when the result falls short.

But their AI use is still episodic. They produce single outputs: a draft, a list, a response. They are not yet connecting those outputs into a complete piece of professional work.

When faced with a complex real task — write a proposal, research a business idea, create a portfolio piece — they either:

- Do it in one long prompt that overwhelms the AI and produces a generic result, or
- Do it in disconnected steps with no system for connecting the pieces

They do not yet know how to design a process.

They do not yet think in workflows.

## Who leaves this Pillar

A learner who completes AI Workflows:

- Understands that real professional work requires a workflow, not a prompt
- Can identify the distinct phases of any real task before starting
- Can assign the right AI to each phase based on what that phase requires
- Knows how to pass output from one AI into the next without losing quality
- Has run at least three complete named workflows and produced real deliverables from each
- Can evaluate the output of a full workflow — not just a single step
- Can identify where in a workflow the quality broke down and fix that specific step
- Has at least one reusable workflow template they built for work they do repeatedly

The transformation is from prompt user to workflow thinker.

# Scope Boundaries

## The practical outcome rule

Every workflow taught in this Pillar must end with something real.

Not a draft.

Not a collection of AI outputs.

Not a document titled "AI Research Notes."

A deliverable: a proposal ready to send, a piece of content ready to post, a research summary ready to act on, a plan ready to execute.

If a workflow does not produce a practical outcome, it does not belong in this Pillar.

## The tool-neutral rule

Workflows taught in this Pillar must be designed to work regardless of which specific AI models exist when the learner runs them.

The workflow describes the type of tool needed at each step (a tool good at research, a tool good at structured writing, a tool good at brainstorming) — not specific product names that may change.

When specific tools are referenced in exercises (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini), they are referenced as examples of what a learner might use for that step — not as requirements.

## The sequence rule

A workflow must have a minimum of two steps.

A single-step process is not a workflow. It is a prompt with a longer instruction.

Most workflows in this Pillar have three to five steps. None have more than seven. A workflow with more than seven steps is too complex to be taught in a single Guide — it belongs in a Mission or Mission Series.

# Guide Objectives

## Guide 1 — Why One Prompt Is Never Enough

**Slug:** `what-is-a-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Concept

**Time:** 5 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to explain exactly why complex professional tasks require a workflow — and identify one real task in your own work that a single prompt has never fully solved.

**Core Concept:**

A prompt is a request.

A workflow is a process.

When a learner asks AI to "write a proposal for a client," they are asking one tool to do research, planning, writing, formatting, and quality control in a single response. AI will attempt all of it — and produce something mediocre at every step.

A workflow splits that request into its natural phases. Each phase goes to the tool best suited for it. Each output becomes the input for the next step. The final result is built from the best each tool can do — not from one tool's average attempt at everything.

The analogy: a restaurant does not have one person who grows the food, cooks it, plates it, and serves it. The kitchen is divided by specialty. A workflow brings that same division of labor to AI-assisted professional work.

**The three signs a task needs a workflow:**

1. The task has more than one distinct phase (research, then writing, then editing is three phases)
2. The output will be shared with someone who will judge its quality
3. A single AI response has failed this task before

**What this Guide must not teach:** How to design a workflow (Guide 2), how to hand off between tools (Guide 3), or any specific workflow (Guides 5–8). This Guide teaches the problem, not the solution.

**Exercise — Critical Thinking, Tier 2:**

Ask the learner to recall one real task they tried to accomplish with a single AI prompt that produced a disappointing result. Ask them to name the task and identify the distinct phases inside it that the single prompt was trying to do all at once. Write: what was the task, and what were its phases?

**Why this exercise:** The moment the learner identifies the phases in a task they already tried, they understand workflows intuitively. No further explanation needed.

## Guide 2 — How to Design a Workflow Before You Start

**Slug:** `design-a-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Skill

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to map any real task into a workflow — naming each phase, assigning the right type of AI to it, and stating the output that phase must produce before the next phase can begin.

**Core Concept:**

Workflow design happens before opening any AI tool.

It is a planning activity.

A learner who designs the workflow first and then opens the tools produces better results than one who starts in the tool and figures it out as they go — because the design forces clarity about what the task actually requires.

**The workflow design process — three questions:**

1. What are the distinct phases of this task?
Every task can be broken into phases. Most tasks have three to five. Name them.

2. What does each phase need to produce?
Each phase has an output. That output is the input to the next phase. Define it before running the step.

3. Which type of AI is best for each phase?
Using Pillar 3 knowledge: assign the right tool to each phase based on what that phase requires (breadth, quality, or recency).

**The workflow map format:**

A workflow map is a simple table.

| Phase | Goal of This Phase | Output | Best AI Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Understand the problem | A summary of key facts and constraints | Research-capable AI |
| Phase 2 | Generate options | A list of potential approaches | Generalist AI |
| Phase 3 | Select and develop | A developed version of the best option | Writing-focused AI |
| Phase 4 | Polish | A final-quality version of the deliverable | Writing-focused AI |

**The minimum workflow:** Two phases. Research/Generate → Write/Refine.

**The maximum workflow for a single Guide:** Five phases. Beyond five, a workflow belongs in a Mission.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Specific workflows by name (Guides 5–8). This Guide teaches the design process. The named workflows are applications of that process.

**Exercise — Hands-On, Tier 2:**

Provide a real task relevant to Filipino learners: "You want to write a LinkedIn post about a skill you recently learned using AI." Ask the learner to design the workflow map for this task — naming the phases, outputs, and tool types — before running any step. Then run only Phase 1 to see how the design changes once they have real output in hand.

**Why this exercise:** Running Phase 1 reveals whether the workflow design was correct — and it usually requires small adjustments. That experience teaches that workflow design is iterative, not fixed.

## Guide 3 — How to Pass Output Between AI Tools

**Slug:** `hand-off-between-tools`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Skill

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to take the output of one AI step and pass it cleanly into the next — without losing useful content, introducing errors, or starting from scratch.

**Core Concept:**

A workflow is only as strong as its handoffs.

When a learner finishes one step of a workflow and moves to the next, something happens that most learners handle badly: they copy the AI's output and paste it into a new prompt without any instruction about what to do with it.

The receiving AI does not know what came before. It does not know the context. It does not know what phase of the workflow it is being asked to serve.

A clean handoff gives the receiving AI three things:

1. **What the previous step produced** — paste the output directly
2. **What this step must do with it** — a specific instruction about the task for this phase
3. **What the final output must look like** — so the receiving AI knows where it fits in the larger sequence

**The handoff prompt formula:**

"Here is [what the previous step produced]. Your job for this step is [specific task for this phase]. The output should be [format, length, quality standard]. It will be used for [what comes next]."

**The three handoff mistakes to avoid:**

1. Pasting output with no instruction — the receiving AI will summarize or reformat without being asked to do anything specific
2. Asking the receiving AI to "improve" without defining what improvement means for this step
3. Including the entire output of the previous step when only part of it is needed — be selective about what you carry forward

**What to carry forward vs. what to leave behind:**

Not all output from a previous step is useful in the next step.

A research step may produce ten facts. The writing step may only need three.

The learner must filter before handing off — passing only what the next step needs.

**Exercise — Hands-On, Tier 2:**

Provide a research output (a simulated AI response containing facts about freelancing demand in the Philippines). Ask the learner to select only the three most relevant facts for a specific writing task, then write a handoff prompt that passes those three facts to a writing-focused AI with a specific instruction for the next phase.

**Why this exercise:** The filtering and framing steps in the handoff — selecting what to carry forward, writing the handoff prompt — are where most workflow failures happen. Practicing them in isolation builds the skill before the full workflows in Guides 5–8.

## Guide 4 — How to Evaluate a Workflow's Output

**Slug:** `evaluate-a-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Intermediate

**Type:** Skill

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to evaluate the final output of a complete workflow against the standard of the deliverable — and identify which step in the workflow to improve when the output falls short.

**Core Concept:**

Most learners evaluate AI output at the prompt level.

They read a response, decide if it is good enough, and either accept it or try again in the same tool.

Evaluating a workflow's output is a different skill — because the question is not just "is this good?" but "which step produced the weakness I am seeing?"

A weak final output is always traceable to a specific step in the workflow.

The research step may have produced inaccurate or shallow information.

The writing step may have buried the most important point.

The editing step may have removed the specificity that made the writing good.

Until the learner can identify which step broke the workflow, they cannot fix it efficiently.

**The workflow evaluation test — three questions:**

1. **Does the final output meet the standard of the deliverable?**
Not "is it good?" — but "would I send this to a client? Post this publicly? Submit this as professional work?" If not, something in the workflow failed.

2. **Which phase of the workflow produced the weakness?**
Read back through each step's output. The weakness usually appears in one specific phase and carries forward from there. Identify it.

3. **What would I need to change in that specific step to fix the final output?**
Not the whole workflow — just the step. Define the change precisely. This sets up the fix (Guide 9 teaches the fix explicitly; this Guide teaches the diagnosis).

**The distinction between prompt-level and workflow-level evaluation:**

Prompt-level: "This paragraph is too vague — I'll ask AI to be more specific."

Workflow-level: "The writing step is too vague because the research step gave shallow information. The fix is to improve the research step, not the writing step."

The workflow-level evaluation traces quality problems to their origin — not just to where they appear.

**Exercise — Critical Thinking, Tier 2:**

Provide a complete workflow output (a simulated three-step workflow that produced a mediocre final document). Provide the output of each step separately. Ask the learner to apply the three-question evaluation test to the final output, then trace the weakness back to a specific step. Write: which step broke this workflow and why?

**Why this exercise:** Tracing a weakness backward through a workflow output is the core diagnostic skill of this entire Pillar. The learner who can do this consistently never wastes time fixing the wrong step.

## Guide 5 — The Research Workflow: From Question to Actionable Answer

**Slug:** `research-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Applied

**Time:** 15 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to run a complete three-step research workflow that turns a real question into a verified, organized, actionable summary you can use immediately.

**Core Concept:**

Research is the most common workflow need for Filipino learners on an earning path.

They need to research job opportunities, freelance market rates, client industries, competitor services, business ideas, and skill gaps — constantly.

Most do this by Googling, reading scattered articles, and forming impressions.

A research workflow is faster, more organized, and more reliable than that approach — because it divides the research task into its natural phases and assigns the right tool to each.

**The Research Workflow — Three Steps:**

**Step 1: Define the question clearly (no AI needed)**

Before opening any tool, the learner writes the research question in one specific sentence.

Bad: "Research freelancing opportunities."
Good: "What are the three most in-demand remote skills for Filipino freelancers applying to international clients in 2025, and what is the average monthly rate for each?"

The specificity of the question determines the quality of the research.

**Step 2: Gather current information (research-capable AI)**

Run the specific research question in a research-capable AI (one with web access, in this curriculum Gemini is the example). The goal of this step is raw information — not analysis, not writing, not organization.

Prompt structure: "Research [specific question]. Provide factual, current information only. List your sources or the basis for each claim."

Output from this step: a set of facts, statistics, and source references. Not a polished response. Not an essay.

**Step 3: Synthesize and organize (generalist or writing AI)**

Take the raw research output and pass it to a second AI with a synthesis instruction.

Handoff prompt structure: "Here is raw research output: [paste Step 2 output]. Your job is to synthesize this into a three-part summary: (1) key findings, (2) what these findings mean for [specific situation], (3) recommended next steps. Use only the information provided — do not add claims."

Output from this step: a structured, actionable summary the learner can read in 90 seconds and act on immediately.

**The final deliverable:** A research summary — not AI output, but a clean document the learner writes from the synthesized response. One paragraph per key finding. Three findings maximum.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Domain-specific research skills (Pillar 5). This Guide teaches the workflow structure — the questions it answers come from the learner's own situation.

**Exercise — Experimentation, Tier 3:**

The learner chooses a real research question from their own situation (a job they want to apply for, a service they want to offer, a skill they want to learn next). They run the three-step research workflow and produce a final research summary. The summary must be written by them — not pasted from the AI.

**Why this exercise:** Tier 3 because the learner defines their own research question and runs the full workflow independently. The real question makes the output immediately useful — the learner finishes with information they actually needed.

## Guide 6 — The Writing Workflow: From Brief to Polished Document

**Slug:** `writing-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Applied

**Time:** 15 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to run a complete four-step writing workflow that turns a task description into a polished, professional document you are confident sending.

**Core Concept:**

Professional writing is not a single prompt.

It is a process — and the process matters as much as the output.

A freelancer who can reliably produce professional written documents using a repeatable workflow has a real competitive advantage over one who types a long prompt and hopes for the best.

The Writing Workflow teaches that process: from defining what the document needs to accomplish (before any AI is opened) to producing a version the learner is proud to send.

**The Writing Workflow — Four Steps:**

**Step 1: Write the brief (no AI needed)**

Before opening any tool, the learner writes the document brief in plain text.

The brief answers:
- What is this document? (proposal, email, cover letter, report, social media post)
- Who will read it?
- What do I want them to do after reading it?
- What is the most important thing it must communicate?
- What tone is appropriate?

This step takes two minutes. It prevents the single most common cause of weak AI writing output: an unclear direction.

**Step 2: Research any facts needed (research-capable AI)**

If the document requires accurate facts — client research, industry information, current rates, company background — run a targeted research step before writing.

Prompt structure: "I am writing a [document type] for [audience]. I need accurate, current information about [specific topic]. Provide facts only — no writing."

Skip this step if the document is entirely from the learner's own knowledge (e.g., an email about their own services).

**Step 3: Generate the first draft (generalist AI)**

Pass the brief and any research to a generalist AI and ask for a complete first draft.

Handoff prompt structure: "Here is a brief for a document I need to write: [paste brief]. Here are relevant facts to include: [paste research if applicable]. Write a complete first draft that follows the brief exactly. Do not add information I did not provide."

Output from this step: a complete draft — functional but not yet polished. Expect it to be structurally sound but generic in places.

**Step 4: Polish and refine (writing-focused AI)**

Pass the draft to a writing-focused AI for targeted improvement.

Handoff prompt structure: "Here is a draft I need polished for professional use: [paste draft]. The document will be read by [audience]. Improve the following specifically: [name one to three specific weaknesses — not 'make it better']. Do not change the structure or the facts."

The learner reviews the polished output and makes final edits by hand.

**The final deliverable:** The document itself — a specific, named professional document the learner can send, submit, or publish.

**What this Guide must not teach:** How to write well (Pillar 5). This Guide teaches the workflow. Writing quality is a separate skill developed in Pillar 5. The workflow produces a better result than a single prompt even when the learner's writing skill is not yet advanced.

**Exercise — Revision, Tier 2 → Tier 3:**

Tier 2: Provide a pre-written brief for a common task (a short email introducing a freelance social media management service to a potential client). The learner runs Steps 3 and 4 only — skipping the brief they would normally write themselves. Compare the output to what a single prompt would have produced.

Tier 3: The learner writes their own brief for a real document they need to produce — something they actually need to send — and runs all four steps. The deliverable is the real document.

**Why this exercise:** Tier 2 reduces the barrier to starting by providing the brief. Tier 3 makes the exercise personally valuable. Most learners who attempt Tier 3 finish with something they actually send — which is the best possible outcome for a learning exercise.

## Guide 7 — The Content Workflow: From Idea to Ready-to-Publish

**Slug:** `content-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Applied

**Time:** 15 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to run a complete four-step content creation workflow that turns a topic or goal into a ready-to-publish piece of content for your chosen platform.

**Core Concept:**

Content creation is the most common earning path for Filipino learners who start with Cyberussell.

It is also one of the most common areas where AI use goes wrong — not because AI cannot help with content, but because learners use it the wrong way. They ask AI to write a post. They copy what it produces. They post it. And then they wonder why it sounds like everyone else's content.

The Content Workflow solves this by treating content creation as a process with distinct phases — and by making the learner's own perspective the non-negotiable input at the start.

**The Content Workflow — Four Steps:**

**Step 1: Define the content purpose (no AI needed)**

Before opening any tool, the learner answers three questions:

1. What is the platform? (LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, blog, YouTube)
2. Who is the intended audience and what do they care about?
3. What one thing do I want the audience to understand, feel, or do after consuming this content?

The answer to question 3 is the content's purpose. Without it, AI will produce content that sounds fine but does nothing.

**Step 2: Generate angles and formats (generalist AI)**

Give the content purpose to a generalist AI and ask it to generate multiple content angles and formats.

Prompt structure: "I want to create content about [topic] for [platform]. My audience is [audience description]. The purpose is to [purpose from Step 1]. Generate 10 possible angles or formats I could use to communicate this, ranging from practical to personal to provocative. Do not write the content yet — just the angles."

Output from this step: a menu of options. The learner selects one.

**Step 3: Draft from the learner's perspective (generalist AI)**

The learner provides the angle they selected, and — critically — their own experience or perspective on it in two to three sentences. This is the non-negotiable input. AI should not invent the learner's perspective.

Prompt structure: "I am writing a [format] about [selected angle] for [platform]. Here is my personal take on this topic: [2–3 sentences in the learner's own words]. Write a draft using my perspective as the foundation. Match the tone and style that works on [platform]."

Output from this step: a draft that sounds like it has a real person behind it — because it does.

**Step 4: Refine for the platform (writing-focused AI)**

Pass the draft to a writing-focused AI for final polish specific to the platform.

Prompt structure: "Here is a draft for [platform]: [paste draft]. Refine it for this platform specifically — consider length, hook strength, and the typical expectations of [platform] audiences. Do not change the core perspective or message. Only improve the execution."

**The final deliverable:** A piece of content ready to publish. The learner posts it or schedules it before the exercise is complete.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Content strategy, platform algorithm knowledge, or social media marketing skills (Pillar 5). This Guide teaches the workflow. Platform expertise is a separate skill.

**Exercise — Hands-On, Tier 3:**

The learner runs all four steps for a real piece of content they want to create for their own platform or profile. The deliverable is a published or scheduled post. No simulated content. No hypothetical scenario. Real content, real platform.

**Why this exercise:** Publishing the content is the deliverable. Anything short of that is not completing the exercise. The act of publishing — with content partially built by AI and fundamentally shaped by the learner — is the confidence-building experience this Guide is designed to produce.

## Guide 8 — The Planning Workflow: From Goal to Executable Plan

**Slug:** `planning-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Applied

**Time:** 15 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to run a complete four-step planning workflow that turns a vague goal into a specific, time-bound, executable action plan.

**Core Concept:**

A goal without a plan is a wish.

Most Filipino learners who want to build a freelance business, start a content channel, or learn a new skill know what they want. They do not know, specifically, how to get there in a way that is realistic for their time, resources, and constraints.

The Planning Workflow turns a stated goal into a working plan — one that the learner can actually execute, not one that sounds good in a document and then sits unopened.

The key difference between this workflow and a generic "make me a plan" prompt: this workflow demands the learner's real constraints as input, and evaluates the output against those constraints before accepting the plan.

**The Planning Workflow — Four Steps:**

**Step 1: Define the goal and constraints (no AI needed)**

The learner writes:

- The specific goal in one sentence (not "start freelancing" — "get my first paid client for social media management by [specific date]")
- Available time per week in hours
- Budget available (including zero)
- Skills they already have
- The single biggest obstacle they expect to face

These constraints are the non-negotiable inputs to the workflow. A plan that ignores them is not a plan.

**Step 2: Generate a realistic action plan (generalist AI)**

Prompt structure: "I want to achieve the following goal: [goal]. Here are my real constraints: [list constraints from Step 1]. Generate a realistic week-by-week action plan for the next four weeks. Each week must have no more than three specific actions. The actions must be executable given my constraints. Flag any assumption you are making about my situation."

Output from this step: a draft plan. The learner reviews it for realism — does each action actually fit their time and constraints?

**Step 3: Pressure-test the plan (generalist AI, same or different tool)**

This step is the most important one that most learners skip when planning on their own.

Prompt structure: "Here is my plan: [paste Step 2 output]. I want you to argue against it. What is most likely to go wrong? What am I probably overestimating? What am I probably underestimating? What is the single most important thing I should do in week one to make the rest of the plan more likely to succeed?"

Output from this step: a realistic critique of the plan. The learner uses this critique to revise the plan before finalizing.

**Step 4: Finalize and commit (learner's own writing)**

The learner rewrites the final plan in their own words — incorporating the revisions identified in Step 3. Not pasted from AI. Written by the learner.

The plan includes:
- Four weeks of specific weekly actions (maximum three per week)
- The most important single action for week one
- One sentence for each week: "I will know this week succeeded if..."

**The final deliverable:** A four-week plan in the learner's own words. Not AI output. A plan the learner actually owns.

**What this Guide must not teach:** Goal-setting methodology, productivity systems, or task management skills. This Guide teaches the planning workflow. The learner's goals and constraints are entirely theirs.

**Exercise — Experimentation, Tier 3:**

The learner runs all four steps with a real goal they actually want to achieve. The four-week plan must be written by hand (or typed by the learner) in Step 4 — not copy-pasted from AI. The exercise is complete when the learner has a plan they believe in enough to start week one.

**Why this exercise:** A plan the learner wrote in their own words — built from AI-assisted research and pressure-testing — is far more likely to be executed than an AI-generated plan. The learner's ownership of the final step is essential.

## Guide 9 — How to Fix a Workflow That Is Not Working

**Slug:** `fix-a-broken-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Skill

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to diagnose which step in a workflow produced the quality failure in the final output — and apply a targeted fix to that specific step without rebuilding the entire workflow.

**Core Concept:**

A workflow that does not produce a good output is not a failed workflow.

It is a workflow with a specific broken step.

The most common mistake learners make when a workflow output disappoints them is to abandon the workflow entirely and start over with a single prompt. This is the wrong response.

Workflows fail in predictable patterns. Each pattern traces to a specific step. Knowing the patterns means fixing the right step immediately — not rebuilding from scratch.

**The four most common workflow failure patterns:**

**Pattern 1 — The research step was shallow**

Sign: The final output contains general statements and no specific facts. Everything could have been written without research.

Fix: Rerun the research step with a more specific question. Pass only the new, specific research to the next step.

**Pattern 2 — The handoff lost context**

Sign: The writing or synthesis step produced output that ignored important information from the previous step.

Fix: Rewrite the handoff prompt. Include the specific information from the previous step that the current step ignored. Add explicit instructions to incorporate it.

**Pattern 3 — The draft step was too generic**

Sign: The draft could have been written by anyone about anything. It has no specificity, no voice, no particular relevance to the learner's situation.

Fix: Add the learner's specific constraints, perspective, or situation to the draft step prompt. AI produces generic output when the input is generic. Specific input produces specific output.

**Pattern 4 — The final step over-polished**

Sign: The final version is grammatically correct and structurally sound — but it has lost the specificity and character of the draft. It sounds polished but empty.

Fix: Rerun the polish step with explicit instructions to preserve the specific details, examples, and perspective from the draft. Or: make the final edits manually rather than asking AI to polish again.

**The one rule for fixing workflows:**

Fix one step at a time. Identify the step that broke. Fix it. Re-run from that step. Do not rebuild the entire workflow unless the failure pattern is in Step 1 (brief or question definition).

**Exercise — Revision, Tier 2:**

Provide a four-step workflow output with a clearly identifiable failure pattern (Pattern 2 — the handoff lost context). Provide each step's output. Ask the learner to identify the pattern, name the broken step, and rewrite the handoff prompt that would fix it. They do not run the corrected workflow — they write the fix.

**Why this exercise:** Diagnosing and naming the fix is the skill. Running it reinforces the skill but is covered in the Mission.

## Guide 10 — How to Build a Workflow Template You Will Actually Use

**Slug:** `build-your-own-workflow`

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Type:** Applied

**Time:** 15 minutes

**Learning Outcome:** You will be able to design a reusable workflow template for a task you run repeatedly — so that the next time you run it, you spend your time on the work, not on figuring out the process.

**Core Concept:**

The four named workflows in this Pillar (research, writing, content, planning) are starting points.

Every learner's real work has its own patterns — tasks they run weekly, monthly, or for every new client. A freelancer who writes proposals regularly does not want to redesign the writing workflow every time. A content creator who publishes three posts per week does not want to think about content workflow design for each one.

A workflow template removes that friction.

A template is a workflow designed in advance — with the steps named, the AI types assigned, the handoff prompts pre-written with blanks the learner fills in each time.

The learner who has a library of personal workflow templates is operating at a different level than one who designs from scratch every time.

**The template format:**

A workflow template has five sections:

1. **Task name** — what task this template handles
2. **Deliverable** — what the completed workflow produces
3. **Phase map** — the steps, in order, with AI type and goal per step
4. **Handoff prompts** — pre-written prompts for each step, with [BLANKS] for the learner to fill in each time
5. **Quality checklist** — three to five questions the learner asks before marking the deliverable complete

**The blanks in handoff prompts:**

Every reusable template has inputs that change each time: the client's name, the specific topic, the platform, the deadline. Blanks capture those inputs.

Example handoff prompt with blanks:

"Here is research about [TOPIC]. I am writing a [DOCUMENT TYPE] for [AUDIENCE]. Summarize the three most relevant findings for this purpose and suggest how each one could support the document."

When the learner runs this template, they fill in the blanks and the prompt is ready.

**Exercise — Experimentation, Tier 3:**

The learner selects one task they run regularly in their own work or aspiring work — something they will do again next week or next month. They build a complete template for it: phase map, handoff prompts with blanks, quality checklist. They do not run the workflow. They build the template that will make the next run faster.

**Why this exercise:** The template is the deliverable. A learner who finishes Guide 10 with a real template for a task they actually do has gained something they will use for years. That is the definition of a successful advanced exercise.

# Mission Series

## Mission: Run a Real Project with AI

**Type:** Mission Series (2 parts)

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Total Time:** 90 minutes (Part 1: 45 min, Part 2: 45 min)

**Required Guides:** Guides 1–9 (hard prerequisite), all previous Pillars completed

**Required AI:** At least two of: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini (learner's choice based on task)

**Deliverable:** A real, complete professional output produced using a multi-step AI workflow — plus a written workflow log that documents each step, the AI used, and the quality assessment at each point.

---

### Why a Mission Series

A single Mission cannot contain a complete professional project without exceeding 120 minutes.

The two-part structure gives the learner space to execute the workflow properly (Part 1) and to reflect on it with enough distance to identify what worked and what to improve (Part 2).

The gap between Part 1 and Part 2 should be at least a few hours — ideally overnight. This gives the learner the perspective to assess their own work honestly before reflecting.

---

### Part 1 — Run the Project (45 minutes)

**Goal:** Execute a multi-step AI workflow to produce a real professional deliverable.

**Step 1 — Choose the project (5 min)**

The learner selects one of three project tracks based on their current situation:

**Track A — The Proposal Writer**
Deliverable: A complete freelance proposal (400–600 words) for a real or realistic project in the learner's skill area.
Workflow: Research the client/industry → Write the brief → Draft the proposal → Polish for professional quality.

**Track B — The Content Creator**
Deliverable: A complete content series — three posts for the same platform on a connected theme.
Workflow: Define the content goal and audience → Generate angles → Draft all three with learner's perspective → Refine for platform quality.

**Track C — The Strategic Planner**
Deliverable: A 30-day action plan for one specific professional goal (launching a freelance service, growing a social media profile, applying to remote jobs).
Workflow: Define goal and constraints → Research what the best performers do → Generate the plan → Pressure-test and revise.

**Step 2 — Design the workflow map (5 min)**

Before opening any AI tool, the learner writes their workflow map: phases, outputs per phase, AI type for each phase.

**Step 3 — Execute the workflow (30 min)**

The learner runs the workflow, completing each step and keeping a brief log: which AI, what prompt structure, what the output produced, whether it met the phase goal.

**Step 4 — Finalize the deliverable (5 min)**

The learner produces the final deliverable — not AI output, but the polished version that incorporates the learner's own editing and judgment.

---

### Part 2 — Deliver and Reflect (45 minutes)

**Goal:** Submit or publish the deliverable and document the workflow for future reuse.

**Step 1 — Deliver (10 min)**

The deliverable goes somewhere real.

Track A: The proposal is saved and ready to send to a real client — or actually sent.
Track B: All three posts are published or scheduled for publication.
Track C: The plan is saved in a format the learner will actually refer to — not buried in a downloads folder.

**Step 2 — Write the workflow log (20 min)**

The learner documents their completed workflow in a simple format:

For each step:
- Which AI did I use?
- What did I ask it to do?
- What did it produce?
- Did it meet the phase goal? (Yes / Partially / No)
- What would I do differently next time?

This log is the learning artifact of the Mission. It is more valuable than the deliverable because it is reusable.

**Step 3 — Build the template (15 min)**

From the workflow log, the learner extracts a reusable template — with blanks where the project-specific inputs go. This template is the one they will use the next time they run a similar project.

---

### Mission Reflection Questions

1. Before this Mission, how long would producing this deliverable have taken you without AI? How long did it take with the workflow?
2. Which step of the workflow produced the biggest improvement in the final quality?
3. Where did the workflow break down, even slightly? What would you fix?
4. Is the deliverable something you are proud of? Would you show it to a client or employer?
5. If you had used one AI tool with one long prompt instead of this workflow — what specifically would be worse about the result?

# Overlap Review

## Resolved: Workflow design vs. workflow execution

**Risk:** Guide 2 (design) and Guides 5–8 (named workflows) could overlap because each named workflow implicitly teaches design.

**Resolution:** Guide 2 teaches the generic design process as an abstract skill — the learner designs on paper before opening any tool. Guides 5–8 are pre-designed workflows with specific phase structures. The learner in Guides 5–8 follows a designed workflow rather than creating one. The creative design skill is isolated in Guide 2 and Guide 10. The named workflows are the application layer — learned by doing, not by designing.

## Resolved: The handoff skill vs. domain writing quality

**Risk:** Guide 3 (handoffs) could drift into teaching how to write a good prompt — which belongs in Think with AI (Pillar 2).

**Resolution:** Guide 3 teaches one specific, narrow skill: how to structure the prompt that passes output from one step to the next. It does not teach how to write better prompts in general. The handoff prompt formula (what the previous step produced + what this step must do + what the output should look like) is a workflow-specific skill, not a general prompting skill. The distinction must be maintained explicitly in the Guide.

## Resolved: Named workflows vs. domain skills

**Risk:** The writing workflow (Guide 6), content workflow (Guide 7), and planning workflow (Guide 8) could drift into teaching writing, content creation, or planning as domain skills — which belong in Pillar 5.

**Resolution:** Every named workflow Guide teaches the process — the sequence of steps — not the skill. Guide 6 does not teach how to write a good proposal. It teaches how to run a four-step workflow that produces a proposal. The quality of the writing is determined by the learner's writing skill (Pillar 5) and the quality of their brief — not by anything Guide 6 teaches about writing. If a Guide sentence begins with "to write a good proposal, you should..." it has drifted into Pillar 5. If it begins with "in Step 3 of this workflow, pass the brief to a writing-focused AI..." it belongs here.

## Resolved: Fixing workflows vs. iterating on prompts

**Risk:** Guide 9 (fix a broken workflow) duplicates Think with AI Guide 5 (iterate until you get what you need).

**Resolution:** Think with AI Guide 5 teaches iteration at the single-prompt level: change one variable, run again, compare. Guide 9 teaches diagnosis and repair at the workflow level: which step broke, why, and how to fix that specific step without rebuilding the entire workflow. The skills are related but distinct. The critical difference is diagnosis: Guide 9 requires the learner to read backward through a multi-step output chain to find the origin of the failure — a skill that does not exist at the single-prompt level.

## Resolved: Reusable templates vs. workflow design

**Risk:** Guide 10 (build a template) duplicates Guide 2 (design a workflow).

**Resolution:** Guide 2 teaches workflow design for a specific task the learner is about to run. Guide 10 teaches template design for a task the learner will run repeatedly. The difference is generalization — a template replaces specific inputs with blanks, adds a quality checklist, and is designed to be reusable without redesign. These are different outputs from related but distinct skills. Guide 2 produces a workflow for today. Guide 10 produces a system for the future.

# Notes for Guide Writers

1. The word "practical" governs every decision in this Pillar. If a workflow does not end in something a learner can use today — a document they can send, content they can post, a plan they can start — it does not belong here. Every workflow, every exercise, every Mission must pass this test before being published.

2. The learner's own input is non-negotiable in every named workflow. The brief in Guide 6, the perspective in Guide 7, the constraints in Guide 8, the real research question in Guide 5 — these inputs come from the learner, not from AI. AI workflows that do not begin from a learner's real situation produce outputs that belong to no one. Guide writers must protect this at every step.

3. The Tier 3 exercises in this Pillar are the most important exercises in the entire curriculum. They are where learning becomes real work. Guide writers must never reduce a Tier 3 exercise to a simulation or a hypothetical. If the exercise does not require the learner to use their own situation, it is not Tier 3 — it is Tier 2 with extra steps.

4. The workflow map table format (Guide 2) must be consistent across all named workflow Guides. Every named workflow in Guides 5–8 should open with a workflow map table — showing the phases, outputs, and AI types — before the step-by-step instructions begin. This creates visual consistency and gives advanced learners a scannable reference.

5. Guide 9 is the most technically demanding Guide to write well. The four failure patterns must be illustrated with examples that are obviously distinguishable from one another. Vague or overlapping failure patterns will confuse learners at the exact moment they need clarity. Test each pattern with real AI output before writing the Guide.

6. The Mission Series gap — the time between Part 1 and Part 2 — is pedagogically significant. Part 2 asks the learner to assess the quality of their own work honestly. That assessment is more accurate after distance. Guide writers for the Mission should include a note recommending that the learner let at least a few hours pass before starting Part 2.

7. Every workflow in this Pillar assumes a specific, real-world scenario from Filipino professional life. The examples — proposals to international clients, content for a personal brand, plans for entering freelancing — are not arbitrary. They are selected because they are the actual situations Cyberussell learners face. Guide writers should not substitute generic or Western-centric examples. The specificity is intentional.
